## 1. Historical Background (Scan pp. 78) — Layer A–D

**Layer A:** Co-operative legislation in India traces from the Co-operative Societies Act, 1904 → 1912 Act → provincial Acts (Bombay 1925, Madras 1932, Coorg 1936, Hyderabad 1952, Mysore 1925) → the unified **Karnataka Co-operative Societies Act, 1959** (originally "Mysore Co-operative Societies Act, 1959").

**Layer B:** The scan states co-operative credit in India has a **114-year history**, inspired by Friedrich Wilhelm Raiffeisen's German model. The first Indian Co-operative Societies Act was passed on **25 March 1904**; the first registered society was an agricultural credit society at Kanaginahal village, Gadag taluk, Dharwad district, registered **8 May 1905**. The 1904 Act allowed only agricultural credit societies; its shortcomings led to the **Co-operative Societies Act, 1912**, which allowed registration of all types of societies. The Government of India Act, 1919 made co-operation a **provincial subject**. After 1956 States Reorganisation, Mysore State adopted the 'Mysore Co-operative Societies Bill, 1957' → enacted as the **Mysore Co-operative Societies Act, 1959**, renamed Karnataka Co-operative Societies Act, 1959 in 1973. The Act currently has **132 sections and 56 rules** per the scan's count (structurally the Act's arrangement of sections runs to Section 132, confirmed against the current India Code text).

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- 1904 Act: agricultural credit societies only.
- 1912 Act: all types of societies could register.
- Government of India Act 1919: co-operation → provincial subject.
- Constitution's **97th Amendment Act** (in force **12-01-2012**) inserted **Article 43-B** (state policy to promote co-operatives) and **Part IX-B** (Articles 243ZH–243ZT) into the Constitution — societies given autonomy, democratic member-control, and professional management as constitutional values; Act amended by **Act 3 of 2013** to conform.

**Layer D:** The 97th Amendment was a response to excessive government interference in co-operative management; it constitutionalised autonomy — hence the shift from "Committee" (implying government-style administration) to "**Board**" and "director" language, and time-bound elections became a constitutional mandate, not just a statutory one.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. Don't confuse the **1904 Act** (credit societies only) with the **1912 Act** (all societies).
2. The 97th Constitutional Amendment came into force **12 January 2012**, not the year it was passed by Parliament (2011) — Act 36 of 2021 itself notes assent in 2011 but commencement in 2012.
3. "Mysore Co-operative Societies Act, 1959" and "Karnataka Co-operative Societies Act, 1959" are the **same Act** — renamed by the Karnataka Adaptation of Laws Order, 1973 (w.e.f. 1.11.1973), not a new enactment.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>When was the first Co-operative Societies Act passed in India?</summary>25 March 1904.</details>
<details><summary>Which constitutional amendment gave co-operatives constitutional protection, and when did it commence?</summary>97th Amendment Act, 2011; commenced 12 January 2012.</details>
<details><summary>What replaced "Committee" and "Secretary" in the Karnataka Act, and by which amendment?</summary>"Board" and "Chief Executive" respectively, by Act 3 of 2013 (w.e.f. 11.02.2013).</details>

## 2. Registration — Section 7 (Scan pp. 79–80)

**Layer A:** Section 7 — the Registrar registers a co-operative society and its bye-laws if statutory conditions are met.

**Layer B:** An application (accompanied by copies of proposed bye-laws and the prescribed fee) is examined by the Registrar against five tests (Section 7(1)(a)–(e)): compliance with the Act/rules, objects consistent with Section 4, aims not inconsistent with social justice, bye-laws not contrary to the Act/rules, and reasonable chance of sound-business success. The scan frames this as five illustrative questions a Registrar asks before registering. If the Registrar neither registers nor refuses within the statutory period, the society is **deemed registered**.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Minimum individual applicants for registration: **not less than ten** individuals (per the base Section 6(2)(b) text) — note some sources show this was proposed to be revised to reflect newer minimums for cooperative-society-only applicants (**not less than thirteen** co-operative societies as applicants, per 2021 amendment) — keep both categories separate: *individual* applicants vs *co-operative-society* applicants.
- Applicant must be **18+ years**, competent to contract (Indian Contract Act, 1872).
- Registrar must register **within 3 months** of receiving the application — if not disposed of in 3 months, **deemed registered**.
- First General Meeting: must be held **within 1 month** of registration to elect the first Board (5 co-operative years' term).
- Bye-law amendment proposals: also disposed of by Registrar within **3 months**, else **deemed registered**.

**Layer D:** The "deemed registration" rule (introduced 1998, refined subsequently) exists to prevent bureaucratic delay from indefinitely blocking the formation of societies — a direct response to complaints of registration being used as a control lever.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. "Deemed registered" applies to **both** initial registration (Section 7) **and** bye-law amendments (Section 12) — a common trick is to ask about only one.
2. Don't confuse the 3-month registration deadline with the 30-day first-general-meeting deadline — different clocks, different triggers.
3. Minimum number of *individual* applicants (10) vs minimum number of *co-operative-society* applicants (13, with State Government able to relax this in special cases) — a 2021 amendment distinction, easy to mix up.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>Within how many days must the Registrar dispose of a registration application, failing which it is deemed registered?</summary>3 months (90 days).</details>
<details><summary>Within how many days of registration must the first general meeting be held?</summary>1 month (30 days).</details>

## 3. Bye-laws — Sections 11A, 12, 13 / Rule 5, Rule 6 (Scan pp. 81–82)

**Layer A:** Bye-laws are a society's internal "constitution"; Section 12 governs their amendment; Rule 6 prescribes procedure.

**Layer B:** Every society must frame bye-laws in conformity with the Act and Rules (Section 11A). The scan lists **13 compulsory particulars** a bye-law must contain (name & area of operation, objects, sources & use of funds, member liability, member qualifications & rights/duties, meeting-calling procedure, Board composition/constitution/powers, staff service conditions, profit distribution, accounts & audit procedures, and matters "essential" to the society's functioning) — bye-laws may also add matters not covered by the Act/Rules, but cannot contravene them. Amendments must be passed by the general body, with a proposal notice specifying the reason, and a **two-thirds majority of members present** at the general meeting.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Bye-law amendment must be registered by Registrar within **3 months** of proposal (deemed registered if not disposed of).
- Every society must align its bye-laws with any statutory amendment **within 90 days** of the amendment's commencement (Section 12(1) proviso, inserted by Act 3 of 2013).
- General body amendment resolution: **2/3 of members present and voting**.
- If the Registrar directs a mandatory bye-law amendment and the society doesn't comply within the specified time, the Registrar may, after hearing the society, **register the amendment himself** (Section 12(5)–(6), inserted 2014).
- Penalty for repeated non-compliance in bye-law matters is covered under Section 109 offences.

**Layer D:** Bye-laws are deliberately kept subordinate to the Act/Rules (they may "regulate further" but not "contravene") so that societies retain operational flexibility on non-core matters while core statutory protections for members/creditors can't be bye-lawed away.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. The **90-day** "align bye-laws with amended Act/Rules" clock is different from the **3-month** registration-of-amendment clock — both use different trigger events.
2. A 2/3 majority is needed to *amend* bye-laws (a general-meeting quorum matter) — don't confuse with the 2/3 majority needed under **Section 14** for amalgamation/division (different section, same fraction — a classic exam trap).
3. Rule 6 (bye-law amendment) is a **Rules** provision procedurally supporting **Section 12** (an Act provision) — a "don't confuse Act sections with Rules" trap explicitly flagged by your source material.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What majority is required at a general meeting to amend bye-laws?</summary>Two-thirds of members present and voting.</details>
<details><summary>Within how many days must a society align its bye-laws after a statutory amendment commences?</summary>90 days.</details>

## 4. Amalgamation, Division, Transfer of Assets — Section 14 (Scan pp. 82–83)

**Layer A:** Section 14 lets a society divide, transfer assets/liabilities, or amalgamate with another, by a **2/3 majority** resolution, subject to Registrar approval.

**Layer B:** A society may (a) divide into two or more societies, or (b) transfer assets/liabilities wholly or partly to another society that agrees by a like resolution. Two or more societies may amalgamate into a new society. No resolution takes effect without Registrar approval; for **co-operative banks**, RBI's prior written sanction is also required (inserted 1975). Dissenting members/creditors get a **one-month window** to withdraw shares/deposits/loans after notice; those who don't exercise the option are deemed to have assented.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Majority required: **2/3 of members present and voting** at a general meeting.
- Option window for dissenting members/creditors: **1 month** from notice.
- Co-operative bank amalgamation: needs **RBI's prior written sanction** in addition to Registrar approval.
- Section 14A (compulsory Registrar-ordered amalgamation) was inserted in 1975, litigated, upheld but procedurally struck down, re-validated in 1978, omitted in 1998, reinserted by Act 28 of 2012 — but that 2012 insertion was **never actually brought into force** (per a Government Order) — so **Section 14A is currently non-operative** even though listed in the Act's arrangement of sections. This is a genuine trap.
- On registration of the new/transferee society, the old society's registration **stands cancelled** and it is deemed dissolved (Section 15).

**Layer D:** The scheme protects creditors/dissenting members (the one-month opt-out) while still allowing majority-driven restructuring — balancing co-operative democracy against creditor protection.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. **Section 14A exists in the Act's text but is not in force** — a favourite "which of the following sections is currently operative" trap.
2. Amalgamation majority (2/3) is the *same fraction* as the bye-law amendment majority (2/3) — different sections, same number.
3. RBI sanction is required **only for co-operative banks**, not all societies.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What majority is needed to pass an amalgamation/division resolution?</summary>Two-thirds of members present and voting.</details>
<details><summary>Is Section 14A (compulsory amalgamation) currently in force?</summary>No — although reinserted by Act 28 of 2012, it was never notified into force.</details>

## 5. Membership — Sections 16, 17, 18, 18A/18B, 19, 20, 21 (Scan pp. 83–86)

### Section 16 — Persons who may become members

**Layer A:** Lists who is eligible to be admitted as a member.

**Layer B:** Eligible categories (current, verified): (a) an individual competent to contract (Contract Act, 1872) who needs the society's services and resides/operates in its area of operation; (a-1) a **depositor**; (b) another co-operative society; (c) State/Central Government; (d) LIC, State Warehousing Corporation, or State-approved institutions; (e) a firm, company, or body corporate; (f) a Market Committee; (g) a local authority. A **self-help group** may also be admitted (inserted 2001, as a nominal member per Section 18, and made a fuller category by 2010 amendment context). The scan's list (individual 18+, area-resident, needing services, not otherwise disqualified) matches the individual-eligibility limb precisely.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Minimum age: **18 years**, contract-competent under the **Indian Contract Act, 1872**.
- A society must decide on a membership application within **2 months** (per the scan) / the Act's amended text (Act 3 of 2013) says **two months**; if silent, deemed accepted at the end of that period for the purposes of Section 105A (appeal on refusal).
- Associate members capped at **15% of total membership** (2014 amendment) — excess must be regularised or removed within 6 months of the 2014 Act's commencement.

**Layer D:** Restricting membership to those genuinely needing the society's services (rather than open, speculative membership) preserves the co-operative's mutual-benefit character.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. Scan's list does **not mention depositors or self-help groups** as members — a current-law addition candidates should know even though the scan omits it.
2. "2 months to decide" (membership application) vs "**3 months**" (registration of a new society / bye-law amendment) — different clocks, easy to swap.
3. Associate member cap (15%) is a **2014** insertion, not original to the 1959 Act.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What is the minimum age for individual membership?</summary>18 years.</details>
<details><summary>What is the ceiling on associate members as a percentage of total membership?</summary>15%.</details>

### Section 17 — Disqualification for Membership

**Layer A:** Bars insolvents, certain convicts, competing-business operators, dual-society members, and (since 2021) employees of the financing bank/union/federal society from membership.

**Layer B:** No person is eligible for membership if: (a) adjudicated/applied to be adjudicated insolvent; (b) sentenced for an offence (other than political/non-moral-turpitude) within the last 5 years post-sentence; (c) carries on the same kind of business as the society; (d) already a member of a same-kind society; (f) [substituted 2021] is a paid employee of the society, its financing bank, its union, or its federal society. A member who incurs disqualification is deemed to have ceased membership from that date.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Look-back period after sentence: **5 years** from expiry of sentence (not from conviction).
- 2021 amendment broadened clause (f) to cover the society's **union and federal society** employees too, not just the financing bank.
- Section 17(2A): if a person is found to be a member of two or more societies carrying similar business, they must retain membership in **one** society of choice within **90 days** of the 2021 Amendment Act's commencement, else newly-admitted membership is deemed to cease.

**Layer D:** These bars prevent conflicts of interest (competing businesses, insider employees) that would undermine the mutual-trust basis of co-operative credit and trading.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. The 5-year bar runs from **expiry of sentence**, not date of conviction or date of release on parole.
2. Clause (f)'s 2021 broadening (society/financing bank/union/federal society) is often tested against the older, narrower "financing bank only" version.
3. Don't confuse **membership disqualification (Section 17)** with **Board/committee-membership disqualification (Section 29C)** — different, longer list under 29C (see Section 8 below).

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>From what date does the 5-year disqualification period for a convicted person run?</summary>From the date of expiry of the sentence.</details>

### Sections 18, 18A, 18B — Nominal/Associate Members, Cessation

**Layer A:** Nominal/associate members have limited rights; Section 18A/18B govern cessation of membership/directorship.

**Layer B:** A nominal member has no share in profits/assets and cannot be an office-bearer; an associate member may hold shares but also cannot be an office-bearer. The scan lists cessation grounds: resignation, death, disqualification, and transfer of shares/interest to another eligible person.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Section 17(2A)-style continuous default: a member who fails to fulfil obligations under the Act/Rules/bye-laws for a **continuous period of 3 years** ceases to be a member (Section 17(2A) as amended, referenced in the scan as a general cessation ground too).
- 2025 amendment: Section 18-B gets a new clause (v) — a person also ceases to be a director if they cease to be a member under Section 18-A(a).

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>After how many continuous years of failing to meet member obligations does membership automatically cease?</summary>3 years.</details>

### Section 20 — Votes of Members

**Layer A:** One member, one vote — with defaulters and certain categories barred from voting.

**Layer B:** No member has more than one vote (except State Government nominees, one vote each). Nominal/associate members, defaulting individual members, newly-admitted members (admitted less than 1 year before the meeting — except at the first general body meeting), and defaulting societies cannot vote. A defaulter is one who fails to pay arrears due **20 days** before the meeting (changed from 21 days by the 2025 Act) after **30 days'** notice (changed from 45 days by the 2025 Act).

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- **One member, one vote** regardless of shareholding (co-operative principle, distinct from company law's proportional voting).
- New-member voting bar: **1 year** from admission (except first general body meeting after registration).
- Defaulter notice period: **30 days** (2025 Act; was 45 days).
- Defaulter cut-off before meeting: **20 days** (2025 Act; was 21 days).
- Agricultural credit societies below **75% loan-recovery** in the preceding co-operative year, that fail to remit dues to their financing bank within **15 days** of that year's close, also lose voting rights at the financing bank's meetings.

**Layer D:** One-member-one-vote is a foundational Rochdale co-operative principle, embedded here to prevent large shareholders from dominating governance — but the defaulter and new-member bars exist to keep governance in the hands of financially engaged, established members.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. The 2025 number changes (45→30 days notice; 21→20 days cut-off) are a live trap for anyone using older material (including this very scan, which doesn't give exact figures here).
2. **75% recovery threshold** applies specifically to loan-advancing (credit) societies' voting rights vis-à-vis their financing bank — not a general threshold for all societies.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What is the current defaulter-notice period under Section 20, after the 2025 amendment?</summary>30 days (reduced from 45 days).</details>
<details><summary>How long must a member wait after admission before gaining voting rights (except at the very first general body meeting)?</summary>1 year.</details>

## 6. Annual General Meeting — Section 27; Special General Meeting — Section 28 (Scan pp. 85–86)

**Layer A:** Section 27 mandates one AGM per co-operative year, before 30 September; Section 28 allows a Special General Meeting on requisition.

**Layer B:** AGM business includes: considering the annual report, the latest audit report and the Board's response to it, any inquiry report, adopting the previous year's programme review, adopting the annual accounts, appointing the auditor, considering the audit/inspection reports under Sections 64/65 and any surcharge order, and profit distribution. Notice (with agenda, date, time, venue) must be given to every member reasonably ahead of time; the scan notes notice may be given in newspapers/post/email/SMS with acknowledgment. A **quorum** (bye-law-specified minimum attendance) is required — the scan notes a 2014 amendment fixed quorum at whichever is lower between a bye-law figure and **10% of members entitled to vote**. Special General Meeting: called by the Board anytime, or **must** be called within **1 month** of a requisition from the Registrar or from the lower of **50 members or 1/5th of total members**.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- AGM statutory deadline: **before 30 September** each co-operative year (Section 27(1)).
- Quorum (2014 amendment): **lower of** bye-law-fixed quorum **or 10%** of members entitled to vote.
- Special General Meeting requisition threshold: **50 members OR 1/5th of total members, whichever is less**.
- Board must call the Special General Meeting within **1 month** of requisition, else the Registrar (or his nominee) may call it.
- Penalty for non-compliance with the 6-monthly-return filing requirement referenced in the scan (Section 27B-type obligation) → **Section 109(19)** treats it as an offence.

**Layer D:** The AGM is the members' principal oversight mechanism over the Board — bundling audit-report review, inquiry-report review, and accounts approval into one mandatory annual forum institutionalises accountability.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. Don't confuse the **statutory** AGM deadline (30 September) with a society's own **bye-law-specified, earlier target date** (the scan's illustrative "25 September") — bye-laws may set an earlier internal target but the Act's outer limit is 30 September.
2. Special General Meeting requisition threshold is "**50 members OR 1/5th, whichever is LESS**" — not "whichever is more" (a very common trap).
3. AGM vs Special General Meeting: AGM is **mandatory and annual**; SGM is **need-based**, called any time.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>By what date must a co-operative society's AGM be held each year?</summary>Before 30 September.</details>
<details><summary>What is the requisition threshold to force a Special General Meeting?</summary>50 members or 1/5th of total members, whichever is less.</details>

## 7. Management of Co-operative Societies — Board — Sections 28A, 28B, 29 series, 30, 31 (Scan pp. 86–87, 90–91 — pp. 88–89 missing)

### Section 28A — Board strength, reservation, term

**Layer A:** Management vests in the Board; Section 28A prescribes composition, reserved seats, and 5-year term.

**Layer B:** *As it stood when your scan was compiled* (pre-2025): Board strength was tiered — 9 members (society operating within part of a taluk) up to 18 (beyond a district), 21 for federal (non-apex) societies, 25 for apex societies, with one seat each reserved for SC/ST and for women in every primary society's Board. *As amended by the 2025 Act:* Board strength is now **not less than 13 members** (excluding the Chief Executive), with the ceiling **left to be prescribed by rules** rather than fixed by the Act itself; office-bearer posts (President/Vice-President, etc.) are now subject to **rotational SC/ST/OBC/women reservation**, and one SC, one ST, and one woman representative must additionally be **nominated** to the Board (2025 Act) — nominated members participate but per the legislative debate do not vote in that nominated capacity.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Board term: **5 co-operative years** (Section 28A(4)) — was 3 years for Boards elected just before the 1997 amendment (transitional clause).
- If no new Board is constituted on expiry of term, the **Registrar/authorised officer becomes Administrator by deeming provision** (Section 28A(5)) — automatic, no separate order needed.
- Current Board floor: **13 members minimum** (2025 amendment) — previous 9–25 tiered structure superseded.
- Reserved seats in every primary society: **1 for SC/ST, 1 for women** (unless no such members exist).

**Layer D:** The move from a rigid numeric tier to a "floor of 13 + rules-prescribed ceiling" formula in 2025 lets the government recalibrate Board size via subordinate rule-making (faster than further Act amendments) while still guaranteeing a baseline of representative capacity.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. Post-2025, quoting the old 9/11/13/18/21/25 tiered figures as "the current rule" is **wrong** — only "**not less than 13**" is now in the Act itself.
2. Nominated SC/ST/women representatives (2025) are **additional** to the elected reserved seats already existing under the pre-2025 scheme for primary societies — don't conflate the two mechanisms.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What is the minimum Board strength under Section 28A after the 2025 amendment?</summary>13 members (excluding the Chief Executive); the ceiling is now fixed by rules, not the Act.</details>
<details><summary>What happens automatically if a new Board is not constituted by the time the old Board's term expires?</summary>The Registrar (or an officer authorised by the Registrar) is deemed to have assumed charge as Administrator.</details>

### Section 30 — Supersession/Suspension of the Board (fills the pp. 88–89 scan gap)

**Layer A:** Registrar may remove a defaulting/negligent Board and appoint an Administrator.

**Layer B:** If the Board persistently defaults, is negligent, acts prejudicially to the society, or the society isn't functioning per the Act/Rules/bye-laws or government directions, the Registrar — after giving the Board an opportunity to be heard — may remove it and appoint an **Administrator for up to 6 months**, extendable by a **further 6 months** for recorded reasons. The Administrator must arrange fresh Board elections before their term expires. Before removing a Board, the Registrar must **consult the financing banks** to which the society is indebted. For co-operative **banks** specifically, if the **RBI directs** removal in the public interest / depositor protection, the Registrar **must** comply and appoint an Administrator for RBI-specified periods.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Administrator's initial term: **up to 6 months**; extension: **a further 6 months** (reasons must be recorded in writing).
- Removed Board members are disqualified from re-election for **1 year** if removed under this section (per the general scheme reflected in the scan's related provisions).
- Registrar **must consult financing banks** before removing a Board (mandatory procedural safeguard).
- RBI-directed removal applies **only to co-operative banks**.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. 6 months + 6 months = **maximum 1 year** total Administrator tenure under Section 30 — a frequently tested ceiling.
2. RBI's power to direct removal is specific to **co-operative banks**, not all societies — general societies are removed only by the Registrar's own satisfaction.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What is the maximum period an Administrator can be appointed for under Section 30, including any extension?</summary>Up to 6 months, extendable by a further 6 months (maximum 1 year total).</details>

### Section 29 series — No-confidence, Casual Vacancy, Disqualification (29B, 29C, 29E)

**Layer B:** Section 29B allows a Board member (other than a nominated member) to resign in writing to the Chief Executive; the resignation takes effect after **15 days** unless withdrawn within that window. Section 29C lists an extensive set of Board-membership disqualifications — far broader than Section 17's membership disqualifications — including: default to the society/another society as borrower; interested in a contract with the society; competing business (self or near relation); acting as legal practitioner for/against the society; being a paid employee of the society or its financing bank (except the Chief Executive); being a near relation of a paid employee; prior dismissal from co-operative service; election-offence or corrupt-practice convictions (barred for 6 years); criminal conviction with ≥2 years' imprisonment (barred for 5 years post-release); and failure to arrange timely Board elections. Section 29E: casual vacancies are filled per bye-laws — the **2025 Act** requires that a casual vacancy be filled **by nomination from the same class of member** in respect of which the vacancy arose (replacing the earlier open-ended proviso).

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Resignation "cooling-off": **15 days** (Section 29B) — can be withdrawn within this window.
- No Board member may be President/Chairperson/Vice-President/Vice-Chairperson of **more than 2** co-operative societies simultaneously (Section 29C(2)).
- Corrupt-practice/election-offence disqualification bar: **6 years** from the date found guilty/convicted.
- General criminal conviction (≥2 years) disqualification bar: **5 years** from release.
- Registrar may additionally disqualify a removed Board member from holding **any** co-operative office for **up to 5 years** (Section 29C(8)).

**Layer D:** Section 29C's disqualification list is deliberately much wider than Section 17's *membership* disqualification list because a Board member holds fiduciary, decision-making power — conflicts of interest that are tolerable for an ordinary member (e.g., being a near relation of an employee) are disqualifying for a director.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. **Do not confuse Section 17 (membership disqualification) with Section 29C (Board-membership disqualification)** — this is explicitly flagged as a common confusion in your source material. 29C is longer and stricter.
2. The "2 societies" office-bearer cap (29C(2)) is about simultaneously holding **office-bearer posts**, not simply being a member/director of more than 2 societies.
3. 6-year bar (corrupt practice) vs 5-year bar (general criminal conviction) — easy to swap.

**Layer F — Active recall:**

## 8. Privileges of Co-operative Societies — Sections 32–39 (Scan pp. 91–93)

**Layer A:** A bundle of special legal privileges — first charge on assets, land charge, salary deduction, tax/duty/fee exemptions, and registration-related concessions.

**Layer B:** Section 32: society's dues from a member are a **first charge** on the member's crops, agricultural produce, cattle, implements, machinery, and raw materials/finished goods (ranking after Government land-revenue claims but ahead of other creditors). Section 33: charge on land owned/tenanted by a borrowing member, created via a prescribed declaration, restricting alienation until the loan is repaid (agricultural credit societies). Section 34: employer must deduct society dues from a member's salary/wages on the member's written agreement and remit within **14 days** (doesn't apply to railway/mine/oilfield employees). Sections 35–36: society has a charge/set-off over a member's shares and deposits; such share/interest is **exempt from attachment/sale** under any court decree. Section 37: State Government may, by notification, exempt/reduce agricultural income-tax, sales tax, stamp duty, and court fees for societies. Section 38–39: exemption from compulsory instrument registration for share/debenture transfers, and simplified document-registration procedure for society officials.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Salary-deduction remittance deadline: **14 days** from deduction (Section 34(2)).
- First charge under Section 32 ranks **after** Government land-revenue claims but has priority over ordinary creditors.
- Share/interest transfer validity conditions (Section 23): held for **not less than 1 year**, transferred only to another member, and approved by the Board.
- Salary-deduction privilege **does not apply** to railway, mine, and oilfield employees (Section 34(3)).

**Layer D:** These privileges compensate for co-operative societies' typically weak enforcement leverage (small loans, dispersed rural members) by giving them quasi-secured-creditor status without needing to go through ordinary civil-court mortgage/attachment procedures each time.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. Section 32's first charge is **subordinate to Government land revenue dues** but **superior** to private creditors — a two-tier priority scheme often tested as an either/or.
2. The 1-year minimum holding period for share transfer (Section 23) is a distinct number from the 14-day salary-remittance window (Section 34) — don't mix the two "numbers" questions.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>Within how many days must an employer remit salary deductions to the co-operative society under Section 34?</summary>14 days.</details>
<details><summary>For how long must a member hold a share before being allowed to transfer it?</summary>Not less than 1 year.</details>

## 9. Conduct of Elections — Sections 39A–39K, Chapter VA; Rules 13B–14D (Scan p. 93)

**Layer A:** A dedicated chapter (inserted 1998) governs Board elections, run by a State Co-operative Election Authority (name changed from "Commission" to "**Authority**" by Act 35 of 2014).

**Layer B:** Elections to Board membership (other than the first Board, or casual vacancies) are held under the superintendence of the **Co-operative Election Authority** (Section 39AA — constituted per Act 35/2014), which appoints Returning/other election officers. General elections must be held **within 15 days before** the Board's term expiry. Voting is by **secret ballot**. Corrupt practices (bribery, undue influence, appeals on religion/caste/community lines, false statements about a candidate, hiring vehicles to transport voters, liquor at election meetings) are defined in Section 39C and, if proven, disqualify a person for **6 years** (feeding into Section 29C(1)(k)). Sections 39D–39K create specific offences: breach of voting secrecy, officials influencing voters, canvassing within 100 metres of a polling station, disorderly conduct, misconduct at polling stations, breach of official election duty, and removal/tampering with ballot papers — each with escalating fines/imprisonment.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Election timing: **within 15 days before** expiry of the outgoing Board's term (Section 39A(2)).
- Canvassing prohibition radius: **100 metres** of the polling station (Section 39F).
- Corrupt-practice disqualification: **6 years** (feeds into Section 29C).
- Elections in **multiple stages**: Primary societies first, then Secondary, then Federal, then Apex societies (multi-level holding of elections, per the 2010 revival-package amendment).
- Cost of conducting elections is borne by the **concerned co-operative society** (Section 39B), not the government.
- 2025 Act adds **Section 39AB**: mandatory declaration of assets & liabilities by every Board-election candidate; failure is a fresh disqualification ground.

**Layer D:** Centralising election conduct under a dedicated quasi-independent Authority (rather than each society's own outgoing Board) was meant to reduce incumbent manipulation of election timing/process — a direct response to the co-operative sector's history of indefinitely postponed elections.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. "Commission" → "**Authority**" rename (Act 35 of 2014) — older material referencing "Co-operative Election Commission" is describing the same body under its old name.
2. Election sequencing (Primary → Secondary → Federal → Apex) is often tested as "which level's election is held first".
3. Election **cost** is borne by the society itself, not the State — commonly assumed otherwise.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What is the canvassing-prohibition radius around a polling station?</summary>100 metres.</details>
<details><summary>Which body currently superintends co-operative Board elections in Karnataka, and what was it called before 2014?</summary>The Co-operative Election Authority — formerly called the Co-operative Election Commission.</details>

## 10. Properties and Funds — Sections 56–62; Net Profits — Section 57; Co-operative Education Fund — Section 57A (Scan pp. 94–95)

### Section 57 — Net Profits and their Disposal

**Layer A:** Governs how a society's net profit must be split between compulsory reserves and discretionary distribution.

**Layer B:** Before declaring/distributing net profit, past losses must first be set off. Out of the remaining net profit, compulsory allocations are made — a **Reserve Fund** and the **Co-operative Education Fund** — and only the balance may be used for dividends, bonus, or other bye-law-specified purposes.

**Layer C — Exam facts (as per your scan; cross-check figures against the current India Code text for your specific class of society, since the Karnataka Act's own percentages for Reserve Fund/dividend are prescribed largely through **Rules**, particularly **Rule 22/23**, rather than being spelt out as fixed numbers in Section 57 itself):**
- Reserve Fund: **not less than 25%** of net profit (per the scan) — this must be credited to the Reserve Fund and **cannot be distributed** except on winding up.
- Contribution to the **Co-operative Education Fund**: **2%** of the remaining net profit (per the scan), payable to the **Karnataka State Co-operative Union**; urban co-operative banks additionally: **2% to Co-operative Education Fund** matched **1:1** by an equal contribution to the State Co-operative Urban Banks Federation.
- Bonus to members: only out of remaining net profit, proportional to a member's business dealings with the society.
- **Scanned material / older position:** the 25%-reserve, 2%-education-fund figures above are the scan's stated figures.
- **Current verified position:** the precise percentages for reserve fund appropriation and the manner of determining "net profit" are laid down by **Rule 22 and Rule 23 of the Karnataka Co-operative Societies Rules, 1960** (rate at which dividend may be paid, and object/investment of the Reserve Fund) rather than being hard-coded figures in Section 57 of the Act text itself — treat the scan's 25%/2% figures as the generally-taught figures for exam purposes, but note that Rule 22/23 is the operative legal source for the exact current rate, which can be revised by rule amendment without amending the Act.
- A **2017 amendment (Act 07 of 2017)** additionally requires **one-fourth of the Co-operative Education Fund contribution** to be paid every year to the **Sahakari Academy (Reg.), Mysuru** for co-operative education, training, and research — **not present in your scan**.

**Layer D:** Compulsory reserve-fund building protects a co-operative's long-term solvency against member pressure to maximise short-term dividends; the Education Fund exists because co-operative success depends on member/staff education about co-operative principles — a "public good" that individual societies would otherwise under-invest in.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. The 25% reserve fund and 2% education fund are figures from the **Rules**, cross-referenced by Section 57/57A of the Act — a "Section vs Rule" trap.
2. The **2017 one-fourth-to-Sahakari-Academy** rule is a genuinely new fact absent from your scan — likely to appear as a "which of the following is NOT in the original 1959 scheme" trap.
3. Net profit disposal order matters: **past losses set off first**, then Reserve Fund, then Education Fund, then discretionary distribution — sequence-based questions are common.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What percentage of net profit does the scan state must go to the Reserve Fund?</summary>Not less than 25%.</details>
<details><summary>What must a society do with one-fourth of its Co-operative Education Fund contribution, per the 2017 amendment?</summary>Pay it to the Sahakari Academy (Reg.), Mysuru, for co-operative education, training and research.</details>

### Section 57A — Co-operative Education Fund

**Layer B:** The Karnataka State Co-operative Union administers this Fund, financed by societies' compulsory contributions, government grants, and donations, and applies it to educate members/directors/employees and the public about co-operation. The Fund must be deposited only in a co-operative bank, a State Government-notified bank, or Indian Trust Act-notified financial institutions; no society may withhold or reduce its own contribution unilaterally, and the Union has no discretion to waive a society's contribution.

## 11. Audit — Section 63 (Scan pp. 95–96)

**Layer A:** Mandatory annual audit of every society's accounts, historically a government function, now channelled through a dedicated Co-operative Audit cadre.

**Layer B:** Until **1977**, audit was a State Government departmental function; a separate **District Co-operative Audit** cadre/wing was created thereafter, headed by a **Director of Co-operative Audit**. A **2012 constitutional-conformity amendment** restructured audit administration further. Every society's accounts must be audited annually; the audit report must be submitted within a fixed post-year-end deadline. Auditors are empanelled based on prescribed qualifications and experience (Chartered Accountant / "Cost Accountant" / departmental auditors with prescribed qualifications), notified by the government; only names, not other personal details, appear on the empanelment list. The AGM appoints the auditor from the government-approved panel. Each society must be audited **at least once every co-operative year**. Every audit report must be placed before the State Legislature.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Audit report deadline: within the co-operative year following the audited year, generally **by 1 September** (per the scan's phrasing — accounts pertaining to a co-operative year must have the audit report ready by 1 September of the following year, i.e., roughly 5 months after the 31 March financial year-end).
- Audit-notification panel: minimum **10 audit firms/persons** must be identified and communicated to each society within **30 days** of the start of the co-operative year (per the scan).
- Auditor qualification: Chartered Accountant, Cost Accountant, or a government-recognised qualification — only **empanelled** persons/firms may be appointed.
- Since 1977: audit shifted from a general departmental function to the dedicated **Co-operative Audit** wing under the **Director of Co-operative Audit** (Section 2AA).

**Layer D:** Separating audit from the general administrative department (post-1977) was meant to give audit greater independence from the same officials who might also be involved in a society's day-to-day regulatory oversight — an internal checks-and-balances reform.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. **Audit ≠ Inquiry ≠ Inspection** — three distinct, differently-triggered oversight mechanisms (Sections 63, 64, 65 respectively) — a trap explicitly flagged in your source material.
2. The **1977** cadre-creation date and the **2012** constitutional-conformity restructuring are two separate audit-related reform dates — don't conflate them.
3. Empanelment lists show only **names** — no other personal details may be disclosed (a specific confidentiality rule).

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>Since which year has co-operative audit been handled by a dedicated audit wing rather than the general administrative department?</summary>1977.</details>
<details><summary>Name the three distinct oversight mechanisms under Chapter VIII of the Act.</summary>Audit (S.63), Inquiry (S.64), Inspection (S.65) — plus Surcharge (S.69) as the follow-up remedy.</details>

## 12. Inquiry, Inspection, and Surcharge — Sections 64, 65, 69 (Scan pp. 96–97)

**Layer A:** Section 64 (Inquiry): Registrar-ordered internal probe into a society's affairs; Section 65 (Inspection): books-of-account examination; Section 69 (Surcharge): personal-liability recovery mechanism for loss caused by misconduct.

**Layer B:** An inquiry under Section 64 may be ordered by the Registrar suo motu or on application (a) from another society holding membership in the society concerned, (b) from a majority of the society's own Board members, or (c) from members holding at least **one-third** of the society's total membership share. The inquiry examines irregularities, mismanagement, or financial dereliction in a society's own internal affairs; it is a **statutorily-timed** process the Registrar must complete within the prescribed period. Section 65 empowers inspection of books at any time to detect malpractice, fraud, or misuse of funds — narrower and more targeted than a full inquiry. Section 69 (Surcharge) lets the Registrar recover, from an office-bearer/employee/past office-bearer personally, any loss caused to the society by misappropriation, fraud, or breach of trust, established through the audit/inquiry/inspection process.

**Layer C — Exam facts:**
- Application threshold to trigger an inquiry from members: **at least one-third (1/3)** of total membership.
- 2010 revival-package amendment: a **copy of the inquiry/inspection report** must be given to the society, and the society must report **action taken within 3 months** to the Registrar.
- Surcharge/dispute-resolution timelines (per 2010 revival amendments to related Sections 69–71): applications to be decided within **12 months**, extendable up to **18 months**.

**Layer D:** The layered scheme (routine annual Audit → targeted Inspection when something looks wrong → full Inquiry when deeper mismanagement is suspected → personal Surcharge liability as the ultimate deterrent/recovery tool) escalates oversight intensity in proportion to the suspected problem, rather than triggering a full inquiry for every minor irregularity.

**Layer E — Traps:**
1. **Inquiry (S.64) vs Inspection (S.65)**: inquiry is the broader, often Board/member-triggered internal probe; inspection is books-of-account-focused and can be triggered more informally — a frequently tested pair.
2. The **1/3 membership threshold** to request an inquiry is a specific, exam-favourite number.
3. Surcharge (S.69) targets **personal liability of office-bearers/employees**, distinct from the society's own liability — don't confuse it with ordinary dispute settlement under Section 70.

**Layer F — Active recall:**
<details><summary>What fraction of a society's total membership can trigger a Registrar inquiry under Section 64?</summary>One-third (1/3).</details>
<details><summary>What is the purpose of a Surcharge order under Section 69?</summary>To recover, from an office-bearer or employee personally, loss caused to the society through misappropriation, fraud, or breach of trust.</details>
